Since before Spanish newcomers arrived, the waters of the Gila River provided for the Akimel O’otham.

The Spanish gave these desert farmers a name still used today: Pima. They diverted river water into

canals they had built with wood and stone tools. The canals ran for miles. They grew corn, beans, and

squash, and also cotton and tobacco.

In the 1800s, so many travelers crossed these lands toward California gold that the people began to see

the river flow change. Settlers stayed in the upper valley and used the water, which hurt the flow

downstream. In 1846–1854 this region came under the United States. Between 1849 and 1851, tens of

thousands of travelers arrived, many hungry or very weak. Here they rested and ate wheat, corn, beans,

squash, and other foods the community shared.

In 1859, Congress established the first reservation in Arizona, about 372,000 acres along the Gila River.

In 1862 the people grew more than one million pounds of wheat, and many sold it. Prospects looked

good. But in the 1870s and 1880s, upstream dams and diversions cut off the river. Farming was largely

wiped out. From about 1880 to 1920 the community faced mass famine and starvation. The federal

government gave canned and processed food by the ton. The community’s history says the change in diet

proved disastrous, leading to extremely high rates of obesity and diabetes — a condition the community

still faces. Jobs on the reservation were scarce, cash crops were lost, and people faced widespread

poverty. The reading calls this the darkest moment in a long history.

The people proved resilient. Conditions began to improve in the 1930s, when Coolidge Dam created San

Carlos Reservoir and a canal system delivered some water back, restoring a portion of farming. Over the

following decades, work off the reservation, small businesses, schools, health centers, and housing

appeared. Income slowly grew, and famine was erased. These trends continue as the Gila River Indian

Community looks toward a promising future.
