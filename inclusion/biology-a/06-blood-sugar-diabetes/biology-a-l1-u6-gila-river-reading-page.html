📖 About this reading

This is a short, careful retelling of a reading excerpted from the Gila River Indian Community. We did not add

new history.

A long time ago

The Akimel O’otham have lived along the Gila River for a very long time. Spanish newcomers called them Pima.

They built long canals with wood and stone tools and grew corn, beans, squash, cotton, and tobacco. There was

a lot of food.

Travelers and a new border

In the 1800s, many travelers crossed these lands on the way to California after gold was found. The people

shared food with them. Later, this land became part of a United States territory. A reservation was set aside

along the Gila River. For a time, the community grew and sold a great deal of wheat.

The river was cut off

In the 1870s and 1880s, people upstream built dams and took the water. The river did not reach the fields the

way it had. Farming was almost lost. From about 1880 to 1920 there was mass famine and starvation. The U.S.

government gave out canned and processed food. The community’s history says this change in diet was

disastrous and is linked with very high rates of obesity and diabetes, a condition the community still faces. Jobs

were few, and there was widespread poverty. The reading calls this the darkest moment in a long history.

Climbing back

The people were resilient. In the 1930s, Coolidge Dam was finished and some water came back through canals,

so some farming could return. Over time there were more jobs, schools, health centers, and businesses. Famine

ended, and income slowly grew. Today the Gila River Indian Community looks toward the future and runs farms

and other projects.
