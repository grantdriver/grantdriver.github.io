Reading

Since before Spanish newcomers arrived, the waters of the Gila River provided for the Akimel O’otham. The

Spanish gave these desert farmers a name still used today: Pima. They diverted river water into canals they had

built with wood and stone tools. The canals ran for miles. They grew corn, beans, and squash, and also cotton

and tobacco.

In the 1800s, so many travelers crossed these lands toward California gold that the people began to see the

river flow change. Settlers stayed in the upper valley and used the water, which hurt the flow downstream. In

1846–1854 this region came under the United States. Between 1849 and 1851, tens of thousands of travelers

arrived, many hungry or very weak. Here they rested and ate wheat, corn, beans, squash, and other foods the

community shared.

In 1859, Congress established the first reservation in Arizona, about 372,000 acres along the Gila River. In 1862

the people grew more than one million pounds of wheat, and many sold it. Prospects looked good. But in the

1870s and 1880s, upstream dams and diversions cut off the river. Farming was largely wiped out. From about

1880 to 1920 the community faced mass famine and starvation. The federal government gave canned and

processed food by the ton. The community’s history says the change in diet proved disastrous, leading to

extremely high rates of obesity and diabetes — a condition the community still faces. Jobs on the reservation

were scarce, cash crops were lost, and people faced widespread poverty. The reading calls this the darkest

moment in a long history.

The people proved resilient. Conditions began to improve in the 1930s, when Coolidge Dam created San Carlos

Reservoir and a canal system delivered some water back, restoring a portion of farming. Over the following

decades, work off the reservation, small businesses, schools, health centers, and housing appeared. Income

slowly grew, and famine was erased. These trends continue as the Gila River Indian Community looks toward a

promising future.
